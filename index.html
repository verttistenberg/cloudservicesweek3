Describe reasons why so many (most?) companies are using cloud based email and calendar services

These cloud-based services offer several advantages over traditional local solutions. They are more cost-effective, for example eliminating the need to house and maintain physical servers. The company IT department doesn't have to manage the whole system, they just need to keep up the cloud service. Also, the costs are reduced to a single monthly fee. The cloud service provider maintains the infrastructure, so the company doesn't have to worry about hardware failures or updates. Though, this can be a significant issue, for example when Cloudflare experiences downtime most web services are affected nowadays. For the employees, there are many benefits, such as access to their data from any device with an internet connection and the ability to collaborate in real-time. OFten these cloud based calendar and email services are a part of the company's digital infrastructure, for example Google Workspace or Microsoft 365.

Compare few cloud data storage provider features and pricing (either for home users or for enterprises)

For home users, Google One, iCloud+, and Dropbox Plus are the three most popular choices, and they're closely matched on price but differ in what they're built around. Google One and pCloud offer the best value at around $4.17 per TB on annual billing, while iCloud+ and Dropbox sit a bit higher at about $5.00 per TB, which in practice means Google One and Dropbox Plus both land between roughly $99.99 and $119.88 a year for 2TB, with iCloud+ also landing at $119.88 a year for 2TB but without any annual-billing discount. Where they really diverge is ecosystem fit and extra features: iCloud+ bakes in Private Relay (a lightweight VPN for Safari) and Hide My Email at every tier, and Apple's setup is essentially automatic for anyone on Mac or iPhone, whereas Google One and Microsoft 365 are the most deeply integrated with everyday productivity apps, since the storage sits inside a suite you're likely already using, and Dropbox tends to appeal more to people collaborating across Windows and mixed-device teams. Microsoft's OneDrive is the outlier on pure storage economics as it's the only mainstream provider offering a plan sized at 1TB rather than jumping straight to 2TB, priced at $99.99/year. Though, that plan bundles the full Office suite, so anyone who needs Word, Excel, and PowerPoint anyway effectively gets the storage for free, even though on a pure per-terabyte basis OneDrive works out to roughly $8.33 per TB, the most expensive of the mainstream options. On the business side, pricing shifts to a per-user model rather than a flat household fee: Dropbox Business plans start at $15 per user/month for 9TB of pooled storage, but require a minimum of three users, which makes Dropbox comparatively better suited to teams than to solo users. Google and Microsoft's enterprise tiers (Workspace and Microsoft 365 for Business) extend the same consumer-style bundling logic with storage, office apps and collaboration tools but at per-seat prices that scale with headcount rather than raw gigabytes.

Your thoughts about when to maintain your own web server and when to use SaaS/PaaS/IaaS to provide web services?

When I'd run a personal web server would depend on the specific requirements and resources available. If I need full control over the server environment, security, and performance, maintaining my own web server might be the best option. However, if I prioritize minimizing costs, ease of management, and scalability, using SaaS/PaaS/IaaS services would be more suitable. I think if I had the time to research self hosting, I would prefer that approach. This way you are not reliant on big corporations, and have full control over your data and infrastructure. Though, if the site had a large user base or required specific performance characteristics, the cloud-based approach might be more appropriate.

List and explain some pros and cons (for example CDN challenges) of public DNS resolvers (Google's 8.8.8.8, Cloudflare's 1.1.1.1 etc.) vs common home networking, where end users are using ISP's DNS resolvers

Public DNS resolvers like Google's 8.8.8.8 and Cloudflare's 1.1.1.1 offer benefits such as improved privacy, faster resolution times, and protection against certain types of cyber attacks. However, they also come with potential drawbacks including increased latency due to geographic distance, dependency on third-party services, and possible censorship issues in some regions. ISPs often provide DNS resolution as part of their service, which can be more convenient but may offer less privacy and control. Public DNS resolvers are multiple times faster than typical ISP DNS resolvers, but they may not be available in all regions and are subject to widespread outages.
